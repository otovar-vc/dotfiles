# Avoid AI writing tells

Condensed from [[Wikipedia:Signs_of_AI_writing]]. Skim before finalizing prose that should read as plainly human-written.

## Banned vocabulary
boasts, delve(s), crucial, pivotal, intricate, meticulous(ly), tapestry, testament, underscore(s), garner, bolster, foster, showcase, highlight, emphasize, enhance, vibrant, robust, landscape (metaphorical), realm, journey (metaphorical), navigate (metaphorical), leverage, align with, resonate with, holistic, multifaceted, seamless(ly), unparalleled, unwavering, invaluable, indispensable, groundbreaking, game-changer, transformative, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, ever-evolving, "in today's X world"

## Puffery / significance inflation
No unearned importance claims: "stands/serves as a testament to…", "plays a crucial/pivotal role in…", "underscores the importance of…", "marks a significant shift…", "leaves an indelible mark", "reflects broader trends", "sets the stage for…". State the fact plainly; only claim significance if it's actually sourced.

## Superficial "-ing" tack-on clauses
Don't close a sentence with a vague participial clause that just restates/inflates it: "…, further cementing its status as…", "…, highlighting the region's rich culture", "…, ensuring its continued relevance". Cut it or replace with a concrete detail.

## Vague attribution / weasel wording
Avoid "industry reports suggest", "some critics argue", "observers have noted", "experts believe", "many consider" — name the actual source or drop the claim. Don't imply multiple sources ("several outlets") for one citation.

## Canned negative parallelism
Avoid "not only X but also Y", "it's not X, it's Y", "not just X — it's Y". Rhetorical filler, not information.

## Rule of three
Don't force triplets ("innovative, dynamic, and forward-thinking"). Use however many items the content actually needs.

## Outline-shaped boilerplate
Avoid formulaic endings and stock headers: "Despite these challenges, … continues to…", "Looking ahead, … is poised to…", "Challenges and Future Directions", "Awards and Recognition", "Conclusion". End when the content ends, don't manufacture a wrap-up.

## Avoiding plain "is/are"
Don't dress up facts with marketing verbs: "boasts/features/showcases/offers a X" → "has a X"; "serves as/functions as X" → "is X". Use the plain copula for plain facts.

## Vague connectives
"in connection with", "associated with", "related to" used to gesture at a relationship without stating it. Say what the relationship actually is.

## Formatting tells
- No em-dash overuse — vary with commas/periods/sentence restructuring.
- No bold spam — bold only genuine emphasis, sparingly.
- No emoji as bullets or decoration.
- No decorative horizontal rules between sections.
- No heading that just repeats the title; no skipped heading levels (H1→H3); no heading with only sub-headings and no content of its own.
- No table where prose reads fine.

## Meta / communication tells
- No knowledge-cutoff disclaimers ("as of my last update…").
- No addressing the reader about the writing/editing process itself.
- No leftover placeholders ("[citation needed]", mid-edit "…" truncation).
- No stock transitions: "in conclusion", "it's important/worth noting that", "overall,", "in summary,".
- No canned assurances in commit/PR descriptions ("all changes verified", "adherence to best practices preserved") — just state what changed and why.

## Self-check before sending
1. Any banned-vocabulary word? Cut or replace.
2. Any sentence ending in an unearned "-ing" clause or puffed-up significance claim?
3. Any "not just X but Y" or forced triplet?
4. Any vague "industry/experts/observers" attribution?
5. Em dashes, bold, emoji, headers — each used only where it carries real information?
